Pax Silica, New Clark City, Capas, Tarlac

Pax Silica would draw more power than the whole Visayas grid, and use up to 1.9 times the daily water planned for everyone living in Makati. Every day.

In July 2026 the Bases Conversion and Development Authority, the government agency developing Pax Silica, put numbers on its Pax Silica campus: 3,000 MW of power at full development, and 65 to 90 million liters of water a day. Both are full-development figures. BCDA puts initial site development in the first quarter of 2028 and says full development takes another 10 to 15 years after that, so every comparison on this page sets a figure for the 2040s against a grid and a city measured in 2026. Numbers that size mean nothing on their own. This page draws each one against something you already know, and then follows what it would actually take to supply. BCDA also puts 130,000 to 190,000 direct jobs on the same project. This page counts what Pax Silica would draw, not what it would return.

Announced figures are labeled with their owner. Everything else is arithmetic you can check, or a result from this project's grid model, whose own sources sit on every chart below.

Pax Silica's 3,000 MW is more than the entire Visayas grid asked for at its highest recorded moment

Start with the number itself. BCDA's figure for Pax Silica at full development is 3,000 MW ("Initial assessment is to be conservative, around, at full development, mga three gigawatts po iyan" was the exact quote), held around the clock here, because chip plants and data centers do not sleep. Full development is 10 to 15 years after construction starts in 2028, so this is a 2040s figure. Put it beside the three island grids on one scale. Two NGCP yellow-alert advisories bracket the same Visayas grid a different way. They forecast the evening ahead rather than record it, at a projected peak demand of 2,482 MW on 15 June 2026 and 2,384 MW on 16 June, against 2,581 and 2,587 MW available.

One route feeds Pax Silica, and it can carry 770 of the 3,000 MW it needs

The second answer is the grid: generate elsewhere, deliver over wires. Pax Silica connects to the rest of Luzon through two 230 kilovolt (kV) routes off one Concepcion to Clark corridor, each route carrying a pair of circuits on the same towers. Solving one recorded day on the public grid map, the most Pax Silica could draw through them at the 7pm peak is about 770 MW, and only one of the two routes actually feeds it. Lose that whole route, both of its circuits at once, and the map shows no path at all. Losing a single circuit leaves its partner standing, so this is the two-circuit case, not the one-circuit case.

The announced fix is real but modest against the ask: a dedicated 230 kV substation for New Clark City, P6.95 billion in NGCP's transmission development plan, which BCDA targets for end-2028, in the same voltage class as the corridor that is already the bottleneck. Ask the grid to serve Pax Silica anyway, and the modeled price of Luzon electricity doubles, all day

Suppose the wires were not the problem. Serving 3,000 MW more means running plants that cost more, and the marginal plant is what sets the price for everyone, in pesos per kilowatt-hour (kWh). This project's cost model replays the same recorded day, 25 June 2026, with and without Pax Silica. Without it, coal sets the Luzon price in all 24 hours, at P6.00 per kWh, the fixed price the Energy Regulatory Commission set in Resolution No. 10, Series of 2026. Neither anchor is a measured fuel cost. That P6.00 is a fixed settlement price the regulator imposed on 26 March 2026 while it had the spot market suspended, and the market reopened on 1 May, seven weeks before the day replayed here. With it the price is P12.00 in every one of the 24 hours, because the day now reaches the oil block at the top of the stack. Oil is the last block the model dispatches in 16 of those hours. In the other 8 the last block is power pulled from the other island grids, or hydro, and the hour still clears at P12.00. Not a spike at the evening peak. The whole day, doubled, for every buyer on the grid. The doubling is exactly P12.00 over P6.00, and the P12.00 is a labelled assumption, so that one number sets the size of the claim: at P9.00 the same run reads 1.5 times, at P15.00 it reads 2.5.

Luzon's whole fleet, the Philippines' biggest grid, grouped into eight fuel-and-cost blocks and lined up cheapest to priciest. A block's width is its megawatts and its height is its cost in Philippine pesos per kilowatt-hour. Coal appears twice because the model splits it, not because two sets of plants burn it: a must-run tranche that stays online overnight is priced at P4.14, and the rest at the P6.00 administered price. The last block needed sets the price everyone pays, so each line stops inside that block rather than at its edge: only part of it runs. The two demand lines are the typical evening, the average 7pm demand across the 105 archived days, not one dated evening. Adding 3,000 MW leaves 664 MW of the stack unused, so the grid clears, barely.

If that cost reached households in full, P6.00 more per kilowatt-hour is P1,200 a month more on a 200 kWh bill (a model figure, not a forecast, and the spot price is only one part of a generation charge). The cables to the other Philippine grids also fill up, and the price gap across a full cable is money someone pays: about P38 million in this one modeled day, with the Visayas evening price pulled up to P11.98.

A what-if on the cost model, not a forecast, and BCDA, the Bases Conversion and Development Authority developing Pax Silica, does not ask for this. It holds Pax Silica at a flat 3,000 MW and ignores delivery. Only about 770 MW can physically reach Pax Silica over the one existing route that feeds it, and even that is an upper bound: solving the whole Luzon network rather than only the lines at Pax Silica's own bus finds more bottlenecks upstream and drops what can arrive to about 530 MW. Real evening prices also already run above this stack. Coal at P6.00 is the fixed price the Energy Regulatory Commission set in Resolution No. 10, Series of 2026, effective 26 March 2026 while it had the spot market suspended, and IEMOP, the Independent Electricity Market Operator of the Philippines, publishes the indicative administered prices. It is a settlement price for a suspended market rather than a measured cost of burning coal, and the market reopened on 1 May 2026, before the day replayed here. Gas is a freedom-of-information answer, but the P12.00 oil price is a labelled assumption, a peaker proxy in the fuel-cost table, where it is labelled an assumption.

Pax Silica's own power station is the actual plan, and it still leans on the same single route

Which is why BCDA says Pax Silica will build its own power on site rather than lean on the grid for the whole 3,000 MW. It did not turn grid supply down. In June 2026 Bingcang put it this way: "We're looking at a power requirement so huge that we will be looking outside of our territory to come up with a power source." NGCP has the dedicated substation in its transmission development plan. Follow the on-site plan at 7pm: say Pax Silica builds 2,500 MW of its own and draws the last 500 over the lines. The bar fills. It works.

Then one 600 MW unit goes out, a generator about the size of one of the two units at the Sual coal plant in Pangasinan. The lines are already asked for everything they carry, and 331 MW of Pax Silica has no source. Building your own station does not remove the grid, it turns the grid into your backup, and the backup comes down the same one route. The 331 MW is set by the unit size chosen: at 417 MW, the largest unit in the outage record below, the gap is 148 MW instead.

The designated site is 1,620 hectares, farmland that critics say grows rice and coconut, and no tree count for it has been published

Power and water are what Pax Silica would consume. The land is what it would occupy. BCDA president Joshua Bingcang put the designated industrial area at 1,620 hectares, and the Manila Bulletin describes the same footprint as a 4,000-acre Economic Security Zone. What grows on it is contested rather than surveyed. Listing the objections to the project, the same Manila Bulletin piece reports that critics say "much of the land under discussion supports rice, coconut, and other food production", a description of the wider land at issue and not a survey of these 1,620 hectares. Hectares are as hard to picture as megawatts, so draw them against the city they sit in and two places whose size people know.

The environmental review is further along: BCDA says the Department of Environment and Natural Resources issued an environmental compliance certificate for the project masterplan, and that each company locating inside Pax Silica must still get its own.
The counts of people do not cover the same ground. BCDA, the Bases Conversion and Development Authority building Pax Silica, says about 10 farmers are directly affected. BCDA has also published an initial list of Project Affected Persons that reaches residents of three Capas barangays, O'Donnell, Aranguren and Santa Lucia, so the 10 is BCDA's count of directly affected farmers and not a count of everyone on its own list. Kalikasan, an environmental network, puts displacement from the whole New Clark City project at 20,000 Indigenous people and 15,000 farmers. Reporting carries that figure two ways: PhilSTAR Life, quoting the South China Morning Post, has Kalikasan's coordinator at 20,000 Indigenous people and 15,000 farmers, while the GMA explainer puts the same concern at "around 20,000 indigenous Aetas and local farmers", one count covering both groups. This page prints the larger reading and names the smaller one here. Those cover Pax Silica's own hectares and the whole city around it, so they cannot be subtracted. BCDA's answer on the Indigenous question is that the land is public and carries no ancestral domain title; Kalikasan's answer is that the Aeta community there sought titles for decades and was refused.
For the wider setting, over 30 percent of Capas, the Tarlac town holding Pax Silica, is productive agricultural land, on the municipal government's own data. BCDA puts the project at 130,000 to 190,000 direct jobs.

An on-site power station is BCDA's plan (BCDA, Jul 2026), and in June 2026 its president said it was also looking outside New Clark City for power (Inquirer Business, 22 Jun 2026) Serving the whole 3,000 MW from the grid instead doubles the modeled Luzon price to P12.00 from P6.00, on a P12.00 oil cost this page labels an assumption

What this page cannot know. The grid operator NGCP, the National Grid Corporation of the Philippines, does not publish what its lines are rated to carry, so 770 rests on a class default of 400 MW per 230 kV circuit and moves if the real rating differs. The grid model snaps Pax Silica to a bus 8.6 km away, on community-mapped routes, not the connection anyone will build, and losing "a route" there means losing both circuits on it at once. Pax Silica's demand is held flat at 3,000 MW from the first day, when BCDA puts full development 10 to 15 years out, and the solar day is cloudless: both flatter the supply side. The price doubling is P12.00 over P6.00, and the P12.00 is an assumption, so it sets the size of that claim. The water equivalences are arithmetic on a household planning figure, not a hydrological study, and no independent study of Pax Silica's water has been published. The 46 million liters shown for Makati is what its 309,770 residents are planned for at home, not the city's whole draw, so it is a floor and the 1.4 to 1.9 times is a ceiling. Nothing here was independently checked, and this page takes no view on what Pax Silica would be worth.
