Pax Silica, New Clark City, Capas, Tarlac

The campus would draw more power than the whole Visayas grid, and use up to twice as much water as everyone in Makati. Every day.

In July 2026 BCDA put numbers on the Pax Silica campus: 3,000 MW of power at full development, and 65 to 90 million liters of water a day. Numbers that size mean nothing on their own. This page draws each one against something you already know, and then follows what it would actually take to supply.

Announced figures are labeled with their owner. Everything else is arithmetic you can check, or a result from this project's grid model.

3,000 MW is more than the entire Visayas grid asked for at its highest recorded moment

Start with the number itself. BCDA's figure for the campus at full development is 3,000 MW ("at full development, mga three gigawatts po iyan" was the exact quote), held around the clock, because chip plants and data centers do not sleep. Put it beside the three island grids on one scale. NGCP's own June 2026 bulletins agree on the Visayas number: evening peaks of 2,384 to 2,482 MW against 2,581 to 2,691 MW available.

The first answer anyone reaches for is solar, and one is actually signed: ACWA Power leased 500 hectares in June 2026 for a farm of up to 500 MW with batteries. So give it the best possible day, cloudless from sunrise to sunset, and count what it covers of the campus's 72,000 MWh day.

So scale the answer up. To average 3,000 MW across a cloudless model day you need at least 12,200 MW of panels. On the land density of a real Philippine build, MTerra Solar's 3,500 MW on 3,500 hectares one province over, that is 122 square kilometers of panels. Now draw that on places you know.

Laid over Metro Manila at true map scale, on MTerra Solar's measured density (1 hectare per MW); clouds only make it bigger. Makati sits entirely inside the square.

For scale: all of New Clark City is 94.5 km², MTerra Solar, the biggest solar build anywhere, is 35. And the 11 dark hours still need about 33 GWh of batteries, 7 times the storage of MTerra Solar.

The two power lines into the site carry 770 of the 3,000

The second answer is the grid: generate elsewhere, deliver over wires. The site connects to the rest of Luzon through two 230 kV circuits of one Concepcion to Clark route. Solving one recorded day on the public grid map, the most the campus could draw through them at the 7pm peak is about 770 MW, and one of the two circuits is its only route in. Lose that one and the map shows no path at all.

Today's lines can bring in only a quarter of what the campus needs
A tank the grid must fill every hour. The water line is all today's two circuits can deliver, modeled at 7pm, 25 Jun 2026.

NGCP does not publish ratings; the model uses the 230 kV class standard (limit 699 to 776 MW by hour), on the public grid map.

The announced fix is real but modest against the ask: a dedicated 230 kV substation for New Clark City, P6.95 billion, targeted for end-2028, in the same voltage class as the corridor that is already the bottleneck. Ask the market to serve it anyway, and the modeled price of Luzon power doubles, all day

Suppose the wires were not the problem. Serving 3,000 MW more means running plants that cost more, and the marginal plant is what sets the price for everyone. This project's calibrated cost model replays the same recorded day, 25 June 2026, with and without the campus. Without it, coal sets the Luzon price in all 24 hours, at the ERC-administered coal cost of P6.00 per kWh (sourced in the model's cost stack). With it, oil is the marginal plant in all 24 hours and the price is P12.00. Not a spike at the evening peak. The whole day, doubled, for every buyer on the grid.

Serving the campus from the grid would double the price of power in this model
Every Luzon plant lined up cheapest to priciest (width = its megawatts, height = its cost). The last plant needed sets the price for everyone.

If generation cost were passed through, P6.00 more per kWh is P1,200 a month more for the 200 kWh typical home (a model figure, not a forecast). The links between islands go from about zero to P38 million of congestion rent in the modeled day, and pull Visayas's evening peak up to P11.98.

A cost-stack what-if, not a forecast: flat 3,000 MW, the 770 MW delivery limit ignored, and observed evenings already clear above this stack.

The market as it runs now prices congestion small and sparse: WESM's published nodal congestion component fires on 28 of 70 sampled days, a median of P0.56 per kWh, at most P19 on 26 May 2026 (the sweep and its caveats).

Its own power station is the actual plan, and the plan still leans on those two lines

Which is why BCDA says the campus will build its own power rather than take 3,000 MW from the grid. Follow that plan at 7pm: say it builds 2,500 MW of its own and draws the last 500 over the lines. The bar fills. It works.

Then one 600 MW unit trips, a generator about the size of one of Sual's two, the most ordinary event in a power system. The lines are already asked for everything they carry, and 331 MW of campus has no source. Building your own station does not remove the grid, it turns the grid into your backup, and the backup comes down the same two circuits.

The 2,500 MW station is a chosen size, not an announced project. Full working in the deliverability note; run any variant in the studio's siting view.

At 65 to 90 million liters a day, the campus would use up to twice as much water as everyone living in Makati

Power is only half the announcement. Water arrives with the campus no matter who generates its electricity: cooling for the data centers, ultrapure water for the chip plants. BCDA's own projection is 65 to 90 million liters a day. Take BCDA at their word and the division is simple. Here is that number three ways.

The small print, exactly: the campus's announced 65 to 90 million liters a day is the tap water of 433,000 to 600,000 people at Metro Manila's 150 liters per person a day; Makati counts 309,770 people (2024 census), about 46 million liters; the pond is sized at 120; the Angat share is 1.6 to 2.2 percent; an Olympic pool holds 2.5 million liters; and as irrigation the same water would keep 520 to 900 hectares of rice flooded for a 120-day season.

Both sides of the argument are on the record. The national water board says local sources can supply the campus "without adversely affecting the local water requirement." Farmer and environmental groups, and Aeta communities on the land, dispute it; coverage puts potentially affected residents near 20,000 while BCDA counters that only about 10 farmers are directly affected. No independent water balance for the site has been published. That absence is the finding.
